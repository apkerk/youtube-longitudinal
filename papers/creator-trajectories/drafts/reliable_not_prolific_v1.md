# Reliable, Not Prolific: Production Consistency and the Scaling of Solo Creator Ventures

Department of Organizational Behavior, ILR School, Cornell University

## Abstract

The creator economy has produced a vast new population of solo entrepreneurs whose product is a persona and whose venture is a stream of published work, yet we know little about what distinguishes the production strategies that scale such ventures from those that stall. The dominant lay and scholarly account treats creator success as an output problem: post more, stay always on, feed the algorithm. Drawing on organizational reliability theory, I argue instead that platforms select for reliable producers rather than prolific ones, so that the temporal structure of production, not its sheer quantity, is what compounds an audience. I test this using reconstructed lifetime production histories for 9,591 solo YouTube creators, assembled from 11.6 million dated uploads. Net of upload volume and channel age, a steadier posting rhythm is strongly associated with subscriber scale: a move across the interquartile range of production consistency corresponds to roughly 57 percent more subscribers, whereas doubling lifetime output corresponds to about 6 percent. The apparent payoff to volume largely dissolves once rhythm is modeled, and the consistency association survives controls for recent activity and holds within content categories. Consistency also predicts per-video attention and protects against venture abandonment, while volume dilutes per-video attention. I develop these findings into a reliability account of creator-economy entrepreneurship and discuss what it implies for how platforms structure entrepreneurial opportunity.

## Introduction

The creator economy has become one of the largest sites of new venture formation in the contemporary economy, with industry analysts projecting it toward roughly $480 billion by 2027 and tens of millions of people now describing themselves as creators. These are not employees and not quite the gig workers of the ride-hail and delivery literatures; they are solo entrepreneurs whose venture consists of building and sustaining an audience around a personal body of published work, a contemporary form of founding that entrepreneurship scholarship has only recently begun to bring onto the field's map (Fisher, Josefy, and Neubert, 2024). A creator founds a venture when she opens a channel, and she scales it when that channel accumulates the audience that converts attention into income and opportunity. What we do not understand well is the operating question at the center of that venture: given that a creator must decide, week after week, how much to produce and how steadily to produce it, which production strategies actually scale the venture and which leave it stalled.

The prevailing answer, in both practitioner advice and a good deal of scholarship, is that creators succeed by maximizing output. The platform-labor literature describes creative work on platforms as an unceasing regime of visibility labor in which workers must remain "always on," continuously feeding content to an opaque algorithm that is presumed to reward volume and recency (Duffy, 2017; Bishop, 2019). Lay creator advice converges on the same imperative: post more, post often, never go dark. This account treats the creator's central problem as one of quantity, and it implies a simple optimization in which the binding constraint is how much a person can produce.

I argue that this framing mistakes the variable that matters. The problem is not how much a solo creator produces but how reliably she produces it. My argument begins from a foundational claim in organizational theory: selection environments favor organizations that can reliably and accountably reproduce their output, and reliability, rather than raw productive capacity, is what such environments reward (Hannan and Freeman, 1977, 1984). A platform is precisely such a selection environment. Its recommender system and its audiences both confront deep uncertainty about which of millions of channels is worth attention, and both resolve that uncertainty in part by reading the regularity of a creator's production as a signal of her reliability and commitment. A steady cadence entrains audience attention and supplies the evaluation system with a dependable, legible pattern; a high but erratic volume supplies noise. If this is right, then the temporal structure of production should dominate its quantity in explaining which creator ventures scale, and the apparent returns to sheer output should largely be an artifact of the steadier producers also happening to produce more.

Adjudicating this requires something the field has mostly lacked: the actual production histories of a large and representative population of creators, measured over the full life of the venture rather than at a single cross-sectional moment. Most evidence on creator labor comes from interviews and ethnography, which illuminate how creators experience the visibility regime but cannot weigh production strategies against outcomes at scale, or from cross-sectional scrapes, which observe a channel's current size but not the rhythm of the work that built it. I address this by reconstructing the complete upload history of 9,591 solo YouTube creators from an inventory of 11.6 million dated videos, which lets me measure not only how much each creator has produced but how steadily, with what acceleration, and with what continuity across the years of the venture's life.

Across these histories I find a consistent pattern. Net of how many videos a creator has uploaded and how old the channel is, the steadiness of her production rhythm is a far stronger correlate of subscriber scale than is her output volume, and the association is large: an interquartile move in production consistency corresponds to about 57 percent more subscribers, while doubling lifetime output corresponds to about 6 percent. The volume effect that looks substantial on its own collapses by roughly two-thirds once rhythm enters the model, which is what the reliability account predicts and what the output-maximization account does not. The consistency association is not an artifact of recently active channels, since it survives a control for time since last upload, and it is not an artifact of niche sorting, since it holds within content categories and the categories themselves explain little of the variance in scale. Consistency further predicts a creator's per-video attention and sharply lowers the probability that the venture is abandoned, whereas volume dilutes per-video attention. Taken together, these patterns describe a reliability premium in creator-economy entrepreneurship: the venture scales when its production becomes a dependable routine, not when its output rises.

This argument contributes to three conversations. To the study of platform and creator labor, it offers a corrective to the output-maximization premise, showing that the structure of production rather than its quantity is what the platform's selection environment rewards. To organizational reliability and ecology theory, it extends a logic developed for firms and formal organizations into the population of solo, persona-centered ventures that platforms have made possible, and it shows that reliability operates as a selection criterion even where there is no formal organization to be inert. To entrepreneurship research, it reframes the scaling of a creator venture as a problem of building a reliable production routine rather than a problem of resource acquisition or pure output, joining recent efforts to map distinctive contemporary forms of founding onto the entrepreneurship map (Fisher, Josefy, and Neubert, 2024).

## Theory and Expectations

### Creator ventures as a selection problem

A useful starting point is to take seriously the claim that a YouTube channel is a venture and that the platform is its selection environment. The entrepreneurship field defines its domain as the discovery and exploitation of opportunities to bring future goods and services into existence (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000), and the solo creator fits this definition closely: she identifies an audience opportunity, commits her own labor and identity to exploiting it, and bears the residual risk of its success or failure, in the manner of the self-employed founder whose career and venture are one and the same (Carroll and Mosakowski, 1987). What makes the creator venture distinctive is that the persona is the product and the audience is the equity, so that scaling is not a matter of building productive capacity or acquiring capital but of accumulating and retaining an audience under conditions of intense competition for attention.

In that competition, the platform functions as a selection environment in the precise sense developed by organizational ecology (Hannan and Freeman, 1977). Hannan and Freeman (1984) argued that selection processes in populations of organizations favor those that can reliably and accountably reproduce their structure and output, and that this preference for reliability can be strong enough to override the apparent advantages of flexibility or raw capacity. Their claim rests on a demand-side logic: the actors who allocate resources to organizations, whether customers, regulators, or exchange partners, face uncertainty about quality and reduce it by favoring organizations whose performance is dependable and whose conduct is accountable over time. A platform's recommender system and its audiences are exactly such resource allocators, distributing the scarce resource of attention across a population of channels far too large to evaluate on the merits, and both have strong reasons to read the reliability of a creator's past production as evidence about the value of her future production.

### Why rhythm signals reliability

Two complementary processes connect a creator's production rhythm to the attention she receives. The first is signaling under uncertainty. Audiences deciding whether to subscribe, and algorithms deciding whether to promote, cannot directly observe whether a creator will still be producing in six months or whether the next upload will arrive when expected. A steady cadence is a costly and observable signal that resolves this uncertainty, because maintaining regularity over years requires exactly the dependable production routine that distinguishes a viable venture from a transient one. This is the same logic by which audiences penalize organizations and producers whose category position or output is ambiguous and hard to evaluate (Zuckerman, 1999; Hsu, 2006; Pontikes, 2012): legibility and dependability lower the audience's evaluation costs, and evaluators reward what they can readily assess. The second process is entrainment. A regular publication rhythm synchronizes audience attention to the creator's schedule, building the recurring expectation and return behavior on which subscription and watch-time accrue, whereas an erratic schedule, however high its total volume, gives the audience no rhythm to entrain to.

This reasoning implies that the quantity of production should matter far less than its temporal structure, and indeed that quantity without rhythm should be actively unhelpful. A creator who uploads a large number of videos in concentrated bursts separated by silence produces the same total output as one who uploads steadily, but supplies the selection environment with noise rather than a reliable signal, and divides whatever attention she earns across many videos rather than concentrating it. The reliability account therefore predicts not only that consistency dominates volume but that volume, holding rhythm fixed, may dilute the attention any single piece of work receives.

### Expectations

These arguments yield four testable expectations. They are framed as associations rather than causal effects, for reasons I discuss in the methods and limitations, but each is a discriminating implication of the reliability account against the output-maximization alternative.

**E1.** Net of upload volume and channel age, production consistency is positively associated with subscriber scale.

**E2.** The association between upload volume and subscriber scale attenuates substantially once production consistency is modeled, because the apparent returns to volume largely reflect the steadier producers also producing more.

**E3.** Upload volume is negatively associated with per-video attention (dilution), while production consistency is positively associated with it.

**E4.** Low production consistency predicts venture abandonment.

## Data and Methods

### Setting and population

I study individual-run YouTube channels, the population of solo creator-entrepreneurs for whom the persona-as-product logic applies most cleanly. The data come from a longitudinal collection effort in which I enumerated a large panel of U.S. creators, coded each channel's operator type (individual, organization, team, or automated), and assembled, for the individual-run channels, a complete inventory of their uploaded videos with publication timestamps. The analytic population is the 9,591 individual-run channels for which the full upload inventory was collected; the regression models run on the 9,583 of these with a non-missing views-per-video value, the eight dropped cases having no recorded videos in the metadata. Restricting to solo individuals is deliberate rather than incidental: organization- and team-run channels are ventures of a different kind, and the reliability-as-routine argument is sharpest for the single founder whose own labor is the production function.

### Reconstructing production histories

The core methodological contribution is the reconstruction of each venture's production history from its upload stream. From an inventory of 11,576,217 dated uploads, I computed, for every channel, the date of its first and most recent upload, its lifetime upload count, and a complete month-by-month series of upload counts spanning the channel's tenure from its first upload to the date of observation, with inactive months entered as zeros. This monthly series is what allows me to measure the temporal structure of production rather than only its total. The median channel in the population is 7.4 years old and has uploaded 652 videos, so these are established ventures with long histories rather than recent entrants, and the monthly series typically spans dozens of months.

From the monthly upload series I constructed the production-strategy measures. *Production consistency* is defined as 1 / (1 + CV), where CV is the coefficient of variation of the monthly upload series over the full tenure; it is high when a creator uploads at a steady monthly rate and low when uploads arrive in bursts separated by quiet stretches. *Upload volume* is the lifetime count of videos, used in logs. *Share of months active* is the fraction of tenure months with at least one upload. *Back-loading* is the share of lifetime uploads falling in the second half of the channel's tenure, capturing acceleration versus deceleration. *Channel age* is the years from first upload to observation. I also flag a venture as *abandoned* if it had no upload in the 180 days before observation. Outcome measures come from channel metadata: subscriber count (the primary measure of venture scale), total views, and views per video (a measure of per-video attention efficiency). ### Models

For the primary expectations E1 and E2, I estimate ordinary least squares models of log subscribers on log upload volume and channel age (a baseline that represents the output-maximization account), and then add the production-rhythm measures (consistency, share of months active, and back-loading). I add a control for log days since last upload to ensure that any consistency association does not merely reflect recent activity, and I add content-category fixed effects, based on each channel's primary topic, to ensure the association holds within rather than only between niches. All models use heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors, and all continuous variables are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles to limit the influence of the extreme right tail characteristic of creator outcomes. For E3 I re-estimate the full specification with log views per video as the outcome, and for E4 I estimate a logit model of abandonment.

### Identification and its limits

I am explicit that these are associations measured cross-sectionally in the outcome, even though the key predictor is constructed from longitudinal production data. I cannot rule out that subscriber success enables steadier production rather than the reverse, since a creator who has already built an audience can more easily afford to produce on a regular schedule. The design therefore does not license causal claims, and I frame the results as the production-rhythm signature of scaled creator ventures. Three features nonetheless make the simple reverse-causal story an incomplete account of the pattern. First, the per-video attention result in E3 concerns an outcome, attention per upload, that is not mechanically tied to how regularly uploads were spaced, so a steadiness association there cannot be a posting-activity artifact. Second, controlling for recent activity directly addresses the most obvious confound, that consistency simply marks channels that are still alive. Third, the abandonment result describes the within-tenure dynamics of stalling rather than a contemporaneous outcome. The honest summary is that the data establish a robust, theoretically predicted pattern and rule out its most mechanical alternative explanations, while leaving the causal direction for designs with within-channel growth panels that I describe in the conclusion.

## Results

### Consistency dominates volume (E1, E2)

Table 3 presents the nested models of log subscribers. In the baseline model that represents the output-maximization account (column 1), log upload volume carries a coefficient of 0.330 (robust SE 0.015), so that on its own, producing more is associated with more subscribers, exactly as the conventional account expects. That impression does not survive the introduction of production rhythm. When consistency, share of months active, and back-loading enter the model (column 2), the volume coefficient falls by more than two-thirds to 0.096, while production consistency carries a coefficient of 2.448 (SE 0.257), and the explained variance rises from 0.068 to 0.107. Adding the control for recent activity (column 3) leaves consistency essentially unchanged at 2.383 (95 percent CI 1.876 to 2.891), and the recency control itself is small, which shows that the consistency association does not merely mark channels that happen to have posted recently. Adding content-category fixed effects (column 4) leaves consistency at 2.486 while reducing volume further to 0.060, so the pattern holds within content niches rather than reflecting the sorting of steady creators into high-reach categories. Consistent with this, content category on its own explains only about two percent of the variance in subscriber scale, against the roughly twelve percent explained once rhythm, volume, and age are included.

The magnitudes make the contrast concrete. Moving across the interquartile range of production consistency, from the 25th percentile value of 0.41 to the 75th percentile value of 0.60, corresponds to about 57 percent more subscribers, whereas doubling a creator's lifetime output corresponds to about 6 percent. Figure 2 shows the consistency association as a binned scatter of subscribers residualized on volume and age, where the positive relationship is visible across the full range of steadiness. These results support E1 and E2: steadiness, not volume, is what tracks venture scale, and the apparent returns to volume are largely rhythm in disguise.

### Volume dilutes attention while consistency concentrates it (E3)

The dissociation between the two strategies is sharpest in the per-video attention results. As Figure 1 shows, moving from the lowest to the highest quintile of upload volume, median subscribers rise from about 8,200 to about 31,100, but median views per video fall from about 6,800 to about 3,300: the most prolific creators accumulate the largest total audiences while earning the least attention for any single piece of work. The regression in Table 4 confirms the pattern, with log upload volume carrying a negative coefficient of -0.509 on log views per video while production consistency carries a positive coefficient of 2.827. Volume and consistency thus pull per-video attention in opposite directions, which is what the reliability account predicts and what a pure output-maximization account cannot accommodate: producing more spreads attention thin, while producing steadily concentrates it. This supports E3.

### Consistency protects against abandonment (E4)

Finally, production rhythm predicts whether the venture survives at all. About 5.6 percent of channels in the population had gone dark, with no upload in the prior 180 days. The logit model in Table 4 shows that low consistency is the dominant correlate of abandonment, with a coefficient of -7.278, far larger than the association with volume. The pattern is stark in the raw data and displayed in Figure 3: the abandonment rate falls from 18.6 percent in the lowest upload-volume quintile to 1.0 percent in the highest, but the regression shows that what carries this is the steadiness of production rather than its quantity. Ventures stall when their production rhythm breaks down, not when their output is low per se. This supports E4.

## Discussion

I set out to ask which production strategies scale a solo creator venture, against a widely shared premise that the answer is to maximize output. Across the reconstructed production histories of 9,591 creators, the evidence points the other way. The steadiness of a creator's production rhythm is a far stronger correlate of venture scale than its volume, the apparent payoff to volume largely dissolves once rhythm is modeled, volume dilutes the attention any single upload earns while steadiness concentrates it, and the ventures that stall are those whose rhythm breaks rather than those whose output is low. I read this as a reliability premium in creator-economy entrepreneurship, in which the platform's selection environment rewards creators who produce dependably over creators who produce voluminously.

### Implications for platform and creator labor

The platform-labor literature has given us a rich picture of the visibility regime that creators inhabit, in which the imperative to stay always on and feed the algorithm structures the experience of the work (Duffy, 2017; Bishop, 2019). My results suggest that the content of that imperative is misspecified in an important way. The selection environment does not, on this evidence, reward sheer output; it rewards a reliable production routine, and the two are not the same. Reframing the creator's central problem from quantity to reliability changes what counts as effective visibility labor, and it suggests that the burnout-inducing pressure to maximize volume that the literature documents may be, in addition to its human costs, strategically misdirected.

### Implications for organizational reliability theory

The reliability argument was developed for populations of formal organizations, where structural inertia and the demand for accountable, reproducible performance shape which organizations selection favors (Hannan and Freeman, 1984). The creator economy is an unlikely but revealing extension, because the solo creator has almost no formal structure to be inert, and yet the selection logic still operates: the audience and the algorithm reward dependable reproduction of output just as the theory says resource-holders reward reliable organizations. That the logic travels to a population of single-person, persona-centered ventures suggests that reliability functions as a selection criterion at a more fundamental level than formal organization, attaching to any producer whose future output an uncertain evaluator must bet on. The point has added force because the creator economy is itself a young organizational form still establishing its cognitive and sociopolitical legitimacy (Aldrich and Fiol, 1994), so that the reliability the audience reads in an individual creator's cadence does double duty, vouching at once for the creator and for the form.

### Implications for entrepreneurship

For entrepreneurship research, the results locate the scaling problem of the creator venture in the construction of a reliable production routine rather than in resource acquisition or output. This complements efforts to bring distinctive contemporary forms of founding onto the entrepreneurship map (Fisher, Josefy, and Neubert, 2024) by identifying, for the creator case, the specific operating capability that scaling requires, even as the occupation of the creator is itself still being defined, much as established occupations have been shown to renegotiate their identity when a new technology reshapes the work (Nelson and Irwin, 2014). It also connects the audience-evaluation tradition (Zuckerman, 1999; Hsu, 2006; Pontikes, 2012) to entrepreneurial scaling, since the mechanism by which reliability pays is that legible, dependable producers lower the evaluation costs of the audiences whose attention is the venture's equity.

### Boundary conditions and limitations

The clearest limitation is causal. Because venture scale is observed cross-sectionally, I cannot exclude that success enables steadier production rather than the reverse, and the results are best read as the production-rhythm signature of scaled ventures rather than as the effect of a strategy a creator could be advised to adopt. The design rules out the most mechanical confounds, since the consistency association survives a recency control and appears for per-video attention, which is not a function of posting regularity, but a clean causal estimate would require a within-channel growth panel that tracks subscriber accrual against periods of more and less regular production. I am building exactly such a panel from daily channel statistics, and within-creator estimation is the natural next step. Three further boundaries deserve note. The population is U.S. individual-run channels, so the pattern may differ for organization-backed channels or other national contexts. The measures derive from one platform, and platforms with different recommender logics may weight reliability differently. And the population is established creators with long histories, which means the results speak to what sustains and scales a venture rather than to what launches one.

### Conclusion

The creator economy invites a folk theory of success built on volume: post more, never stop, win the algorithm. The production histories of nearly ten thousand creators tell a different story, in which steadiness rather than quantity is what compounds an audience, and in which ventures fail when their rhythm breaks rather than when their output is merely low. Reliability, the property that organizational theory long ago identified as the thing selection environments reward, turns out to organize success even in the most informal of ventures, where a single person publishing on a schedule is, in the end, running a reliable organization of one.
